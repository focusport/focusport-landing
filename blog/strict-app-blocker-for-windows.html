Strict App Blocker for Windows: 6 Escape Routes and How to Close Them

FocusPort Team • Published Oct 26, 2026 • 7 min read

Search for a "strict" blocker on Windows and you will find plenty of tools that stop working the moment you get bored. The reason is rarely the blocklist. It is that Windows gives you half a dozen ways to walk around any program, and most blockers only defend one or two of them.

This guide lists the six routes people actually use to escape a focus session on a PC, what a blocker has to do to close each one, and how to test a tool yourself in five minutes before you trust it with an exam week.

The six escape routes, and what closing each one takes

Escape routeWhy it works on a weak blockerWhat a strict blocker must do
End the process in Task ManagerThe blocker is just a program, so it can be ended like oneDisable Task Manager for the session and restart any part that is stopped
Uninstall it from SettingsInstalled Apps is one click awayKeep Settings and Control Panel out of reach while the session runs
Switch to another Windows accountOnly the signed-in profile is coveredHide or block fast user switching
Restart the PCThe session timer or the block does not survive a rebootSave the session to disk and re-apply the lock at startup, before you open anything
Open an app that is not on the listBlocklists only know the apps you thought ofOffer an allowlist mode where everything else is blocked
Hide behind a Store appMicrosoft Store apps share a host frame, so the real app name is hiddenLook inside the frame to identify the actual app

Why blocklists lose to allowlists

A blocklist assumes you can predict your own distractions. You block YouTube, so you open a second browser. You block that browser, so you open a web game inside a document editor. Every gap is a new decision, and decisions are exactly what a focus session is meant to remove.

An allowlist turns the question around. You name the three or four tools the task needs, such as an editor, a PDF reader and a calculator, and the rest of the PC is closed for the session. Nothing new can slip in because nothing new is allowed. The cost is a little setup time, which is why many people use a blocklist for light work and an allowlist for exams and deadlines.

A five-minute test before you trust any Windows blocker

  1. Start a 10-minute session and press Ctrl+Shift+Esc. Does Task Manager open?
  2. Open Settings and go to Installed Apps. Can you reach the uninstall button?
  3. Press Win+L and see whether another account is offered.
  4. Restart the PC in the middle of the session. Is the lock back when you sign in, and is the time left correct?
  5. Open an app that is not on your list, ideally one from the Microsoft Store. Is it caught?

If a tool fails more than one of these, it is a reminder, not a blocker. Do the test with a short session so a failure costs you nothing.

What no blocker can do

Be honest about the limits. A second device, such as your phone, is still a way out, which is why FocusPort syncs sessions between your PC and your Android phone. And someone who is determined to take their own computer apart, for example by booting from another drive, can do it. A strict blocker is built for the impulsive 2 a.m. moment, not for a determined attack on your own machine. That moment is where most sessions are lost.

Choosing a session length

Start with 45 to 90 minutes. Strictness works best when the end is in sight: if a lock feels endless you will resent it, and a resentful user finds the one loophole that works. Schedule recurring blocks for the hours you know are weak, and keep the allowlist small.
